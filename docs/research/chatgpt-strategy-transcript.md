# Building an AI-run game studio: dozens of games, one human overseer

I think this is a genuinely interesting direction to investigate. But I would approach it differently from a typical indie game studio.

We're not trying to spend two years making one masterpiece. We're trying to build a **repeatable game-production system** that can develop, test, publish, measure, and improve multiple games, with you supervising the operation rather than personally making the art, coding mechanics, or animating characters.

There is one major distinction we need to get right:

**AI can produce much of a game's code and content. That does not mean AI can reliably produce a fun game, verify every possible bug, or acquire players automatically.** Those need separate systems, measurable acceptance criteria, and real-world testing.

The business we're building is therefore two things:

- A game factory that produces playable, polished games.
- A distribution and analytics factory that identifies which games deserve more investment.

# 4. The game categories I'd avoid initially

Some games sound attractive because they can generate lots of content, but are poorly suited to your hands-off requirement.

- **Competitive multiplayer shooters:** networking, anti-cheat, matchmaking, and balance are expensive to get right.
- **Massive open-world RPGs:** huge asset requirements, narrative continuity, and integration complexity.
- **Narrative-heavy games with thousands of branching scenes:** difficult to maintain consistency and test every branch.
- **Live-service games with complex online economies:** ongoing balancing, security, moderation, and support.
- **Near-identical AI reskins:** weak differentiation and potential platform-policy problems.

The goal is not maximum output. It is maximum *commercially useful output per dollar and per unit of oversight*.

# 4. The game categories I'd avoid initially

Some games sound attractive because they can generate lots of content, but are poorly suited to your hands-off requirement.

- **Competitive multiplayer shooters:** networking, anti-cheat, matchmaking, and balance are expensive to get right.
- **Massive open-world RPGs:** huge asset requirements, narrative continuity, and integration complexity.
- **Narrative-heavy games with thousands of branching scenes:** difficult to maintain consistency and test every branch.
- **Live-service games with complex online economies:** ongoing balancing, security, moderation, and support.
- **Near-identical AI reskins:** weak differentiation and potential platform-policy problems.

The goal is not maximum output. It is maximum *commercially useful output per dollar and per unit of oversight*.

## 6. Can AI really produce the entire portfolio?

For these genres, much of the work can be automated. Here's the realistic breakdown:

| Work | Automation potential |
|---|---|
| Rules, economy, upgrades, and card effects | Very high |
| UI, menus, saving, and progression | High |
| 2D illustrations, icons, and card frames | High, with consistency checks |
| Animation and sound | High for simple effects |
| Balance simulations and regression tests | High |
| Level/card generation and rule validation | High |
| Finding out whether the game is genuinely fun | Limited |
| Acquiring players and building an audience | Partial |

Card games are particularly suitable for automated testing. We can simulate thousands of matches, check whether certain cards dominate, test whether progression becomes impossible, and verify that every generated card follows the rules. But those tests cannot reliably tell us whether a human will find the decisions exciting.

There are also publishing constraints. Steam requires disclosure of AI-generated content shipped with a game and reviews that content under its normal content rules. <Cite refs={["turn700368search0"]} /> We should create original, curated games with clear quality standards—not flood stores with near-identical AI releases.
